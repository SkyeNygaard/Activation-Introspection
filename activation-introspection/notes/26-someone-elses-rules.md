# 26 — Someone else's rules (pre-run note)

Written before anything ran. Nothing above the line will be edited after seeing
results.

## Why this, and why now

Frontier mode. Stepping back from the introspection line rather than continuing
it, because that line has just closed three of its own questions in a row
([`22`](22-the-weak-arm-was-a-floor-not-a-frontier.md),
[`23`](23-held-out-semantic-generalization.md),
[`24`](24-is-the-held-out-failure-the-interface.md)) and a literature read
established that its conclusion was published elsewhere first.

After two literature searches, **exactly one result in this repository is still
unclaimed by anyone else**: that measuring how well a hidden rule's two classes
clump together in the model's representations predicts whether the four-shot
interface can learn that rule ([`16`](16-visible-rule-capacity.md),
[`19`](19-clustering-predicts-learnability.md)). It is also the least about
introspection, which is worth saying out loud.

`19` scored 12 of 14 with the predictions frozen to disk before any accuracy
existed. It is the only prospective test here. And it has one obvious weak point,
named in the handoff rather than discovered later:

> **I wrote all fourteen rules.**

Someone choosing both the rules and the hypothesis can produce a confirming set
without ever intending to. Nothing in `19` rules that out. This does.

## What I am about to do

Repeat `19` exactly, changing **one thing: who wrote the rules.**

Fourteen new rules were generated by a different model through the Codex
command-line tool, in a read-only sandbox with Skye's global instruction files
excluded from its context. The prompt asked for binary word-class distinctions
spanning meaning, spelling, grammar, number and sound, at varied difficulty, and
listed `19`'s fourteen names as already-used so they would not be repeated.

**The prompt says nothing about clustering, representations, separation,
learnability, or what the rules are for.** The author is blind to the hypothesis.
That is the whole point; a rule set written by something that knew what was being
tested would be no better than one I wrote myself. The exact prompt is saved
alongside the protocol.

I checked all fourteen distinctions myself for correctness — a mislabelled class
would poison the test — and all fourteen are right. That check is about whether
the rule is *true*, not about whether it will pass, and it happened before any
measurement.

The set that came back:

| kind | rules |
|---|---|
| meaning | `reptile_vs_amphibian`, `flightless_vs_flying_bird`, `renewable_vs_fossil_source` |
| spelling | `spelling_palindrome`, `all_vowels_present`, `alphabetical_letters` |
| grammatical | `comparative_vs_superlative`, `nominative_vs_accusative`, `regular_vs_irregular_past` |
| numeric | `even_vs_odd`, `perfect_square`, `fibonacci_number` |
| sound | `silent_initial`, `stress_position` |

Two things about this set are worth noting before it is run. It contains a
**sound** category, which `19` had none of — pronunciation is neither what a word
means nor how it is spelled, so it is a genuinely new kind of distinction for the
measure to be wrong about. And several of the spelling rules
(`alphabetical_letters`, `all_vowels_present`) are harder than anything in `19`.

## What is frozen, and what is not

**The thresholds do not move.** Separation ≥ 0.020 predicts learnable; accuracy
≥ 0.60 counts as learned. Both were fixed in `19` from `16`'s empty gaps, and the
replication script imports them from `19`'s module rather than restating them, so
they cannot drift. This is an out-of-sample test of an already-registered rule,
not a new fit.

Same model, same three layers, same four-shot interface, same 24-cell design,
same scoring. Phase 1 measures separation and writes predictions to disk; phase 2
refuses to run without that file.

## The metric I am pre-committing to, and why it is not 12/14

`19` says this about its own headline, and it is the most useful sentence in the
note:

> Ten of the fourteen rules turned out learnable, so predicting "learnable" for
> everything scores 10 of 14. The measure beats that by two. As a binary
> classifier the separation measure is only modestly better than guessing the
> majority class.

So the binary count is a weak instrument that depends on how the outcome classes
happen to balance — which I do not control and cannot know in advance. **Primary
metric here is the rank agreement between separation and accuracy across the
fourteen rules**, which `19` measured at 0.785 and which is what its continuous
table actually shows. The binary count is reported second, always next to the
majority-class baseline for this set.

Declaring this now, before the numbers exist, is the point. Choosing afterwards
whichever of the two looks better is the exact error `13` and `15` made.

## What each outcome would mean

**Rank agreement holds near `19`'s 0.785, on rules I did not write.** The
relationship survives its most obvious threat. This is the outcome that would let
the result be described as a usable design tool rather than a description of one
rule set, and it is the only unclaimed thing here.

**Rank agreement collapses toward zero.** `19` was a property of my rule-writing,
not of the model. The one surviving novelty candidate dies, and it dies before an
application quotes it rather than after. **This is a good outcome to buy for a few
hundred forward passes**, and it is why the run is worth doing now rather than
after the deadline.

**It holds for meaning and spelling and fails for sound.** The measure tracks
something narrower than "any hidden class". That is a boundary, not a refutation,
and it would say where the gate may be used.

**The positive/negative asymmetry reappears** — positive separation reliably
meaning learnable, negative meaning little. `19` found 8 for 8 and 4 of 6 and
concluded "green light, not veto". A second independent set showing the same
asymmetry would make that the honest form of the claim.

## Prediction, on the record

I expect **rank agreement between 0.5 and 0.8, lower than `19`'s 0.785**, and the
binary count to land at **10 or 11 of 14**.

Two reasons for expecting a drop. `19`'s rules were written by someone holding the
hypothesis, so some regression is likely even with no bias at all — the set was
tuned to span the space the measure cares about. And this set is harder: three
spelling rules of a difficulty `19` did not include, plus a whole category
(sound) the measure has never been tested on.

Specifically, I expect the **sound** rules to be the misses. Pronunciation is not
obviously carried by whole-state similarity at these layers, but `silent_initial`
and `stress_position` are both learnable by a competent model from two examples,
so I expect low separation and decent accuracy — predicted-not-learnable, observed
learnable. If that is what happens it is the same shape as `19`'s
`singular_vs_plural` miss.

## Cost

Phase 1: about 200 short forward passes, no interventions, no prompting — under
two minutes. Phase 2: 14 rules × 24 cells, prompting only, single forward per
cell — a few minutes. **Inference only. No training. No new bank.**

**It cannot run in parallel with `25`.** One GPU, and the standing rule on this
machine is never two model jobs at once; a job was killed mid-load that way. So
the rule generation, the correctness check, the protocol and this note are done
now, and both phases run when `25` releases the GPU.

## What would change my mind about running it at all

If the fourteen rules had come back with wrong distinctions, or heavily
overlapping `19`'s, the set would be worthless and the right move would be to
regenerate rather than measure. Checked: all correct, no name reused, and only two
words (`walked`, `painted`) appear in both sets, in a rule with a different
distinction.
